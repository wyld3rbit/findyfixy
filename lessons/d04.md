# Day 4. Memory II: hybrid retrieval

**Outcome:** a retriever that fuses keyword and vector search, reranks with a cross-encoder, and beats vector-only on recall@5 by a number you measured.

## Concept (25 min)

**Dense and lexical retrieval fail differently.** Dense retrieval matches meaning: "auth is slow" finds "login latency regression". It is weak on exact tokens. Error codes, hostnames and ticket IDs are rare strings that the embedding smooths into their neighbourhood, so `ERR_4471` and `ERR_4417` land in nearly the same place.

Lexical retrieval with BM25 is the opposite. It scores term overlap. Each matching term contributes its inverse document frequency (rare terms count more), with term frequency saturating (parameter `k1`) and long documents normalised down (parameter `b`). It finds `ERR_4471` exactly and misses every paraphrase. Production retrievers run both and merge the results.

**Merging needs care, because the scores are not comparable.** A BM25 score of 12 and a cosine of 0.83 live on different scales with different distributions. Normalising and weighting them is brittle and needs re-tuning whenever the corpus changes.

Reciprocal-rank fusion ignores scores and uses ranks: `score(d) = Σ 1 / (k + rank_i(d))` across the lists. A document near the top of both lists wins. The constant `k = 60` flattens the curve so that rank 1 does not dominate rank 5. It has no training step and few failure modes, which is why it is the default.

**Reranking.** A bi-encoder embeds query and document separately, so it never sees them together. A cross-encoder reads the query and the document as one input and attends across both, which makes it much better at judging relevance. It also costs one forward pass per candidate per query. So the pattern is two stages: retrieve 30 to 50 candidates cheaply, then rerank only those. The rerank stage is usually the largest slice of the retrieval latency budget.

**Measure retrieval on its own.** If the right incident never reaches the prompt, no prompt engineering recovers it. More context is not safer either: irrelevant chunks distract the model and cost tokens.

- **Recall@k:** did a correct item appear in the top k?
- **MRR (mean reciprocal rank):** how high did the first correct item rank? Rank 1 scores 1, rank 4 scores 0.25.
- **nDCG:** when there are several correct items with graded relevance.

Both need a labelled set: queries paired with the items that should come back. Without one, retrieval quality is opinion.

### What the table says

- Exact tokens: recall@5 goes 0.70 (vector), 0.90 (hybrid), 1.00 (rerank). Vector missed 3 of 10 hostnames in the top 5 (t03 at rank 10, t06 at rank 9, t09 at rank 8). BM25 finds them. This is the point of hybrid.
- Meaning queries: all three have recall@5 of 1.00. Hybrid and rerank do not improve it. They slightly lower MRR (0.864, 0.828, 0.763) and all@5 (0.73, 0.60, 0.57).
- Why meaning gets worse, from the per-query ranks (assistant diagnostic, not saved): each failure pattern has nine originals (different services and numbers), plus any seeded twins. A paraphrase matches all of them. The twin's partner is often outranked by other incidents of the same pattern, at ranks 4 to 9. That is retrieval working on meaning, but the label only counts the twin, so all@5 and MRR punish it. The labels measure "find this exact incident", and the queries ask "find incidents like this".
- Rerank costs about 340 ms per query against about 18 ms. That is 20 times the latency for 0.07 of overall recall@5 (0.93 to 1.00), all of it on token queries.
- t06 (`notifications-001`): hybrid ranked it outside the top 10 (BM25 weight diluted), the reranker, which sees the top 30 chunks, put it at rank 1. A rerank stage can rescue a candidate that fusion left low, but only if it is in the pool.

### Limits of this measurement

- 40 queries. One query is 2.5 points. Differences of a few points are noise.
- Templated data, one writer for the meaning queries.
- Latency is a median over 40 calls on a laptop CPU, with Ollama idle.

### Self-check item 3 and drill (written by Claude at the learner's request, 2026-11-08)

The learner asked Claude to answer these. They are not the learner's own recall.

- RRF in one minute: vector search and BM25 each return a ranked list, and their scores sit on different scales (a BM25 score of 12 against a cosine distance of 0.17), so they cannot be added. Reciprocal rank fusion ignores the scores and uses ranks: each chunk scores the sum over both lists of 1 / (60 + rank). A chunk high in both lists wins. The 60 flattens the curve, so rank 1 is worth only a little more than rank 5. It needs no training and no weights to tune. In this project: BM25 top 50 and vector top 50, k = 60, then the reranker takes the top 30.
- Drill, "Your RAG system gives confident wrong answers. Where do you look first?": retrieval, before generation. If the right incident never reaches the prompt, no prompt fixes it. Measure recall on a labelled set first. Here, vector search missed 3 of 10 exact-hostname queries in the top 5 (recall@5 0.70); BM25 with fusion brought it to 0.90 and the reranker to 1.00, at about 340 ms against 14 ms. Then check what reaches the prompt: for one alert, 2 of the 3 slots held the same incident told twice.
